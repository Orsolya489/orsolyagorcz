A multimodal signalling system that makes AI uncertainty, ethical friction and bias perceptible in real time — before the user has already acted on the answer.

The system never says “this is wrong.” It says “something here needs you.”

Read this if you read nothing else.

Chat AI communicates in exactly one channel: text. So when the model is guessing, drifting off the brief, or wandering into territory that deserves more care than a sentence can carry, it has only one way to tell you — more text. And more text reads exactly like the confident text around it.

I ran an independent study with 83 people to find out whether that gap actually costs anyone anything, and what a fix would have to look like to be tolerable. It does, and the answer was narrower than I expected: people don’t want a second opinion, and they don’t want a warning label. 84% said interpreting a sensitive or uncertain answer is the human’s job, not the AI’s. What they want is a notice — a cue that something in the last answer has a different weight to it — delivered quietly enough that they can ignore it.

So I designed a signalling layer instead of an advisory one. Three kinds of friction, three sensory channels, no verdicts. It borrows its interaction model from lane departure warning in cars: the steering wheel buzzes, you look, you decide. The car never turns the wheel.

The model stops doing what you asked, and nothing on screen changes.

FILL — replace with the real first moment: the specific conversation, the specific task. One scene, not a category. Small and concrete. What follows is written to receive it.

It’s the same shape every time. You’re an hour into something — a long piece of writing, a decision you’re working through, a problem with real stakes. The answers keep arriving in the same even, well-formed voice. And at some point the model quietly stops doing the thing you asked. It drops the constraint you set at the start. It starts agreeing with a premise you were half-testing. It states something as fact that it does not have.

None of that looks different on screen.

You find out later. You find out when you re-read, or when someone else reads it, or when you’ve already sent it. One respondent described this without being prompted, in an open text box, when I asked what one thing they would improve:

“Consistency, and a signal from the AI when it loses focus. You only find out afterwards that it stopped following the instructions it was given for the task, that it dropped the character — but it never signalled it.”

Respondent, Hungarian sample, age 35–44

That is a person specifying this product in a survey box, unprompted, before I had described it to anyone.

Confidence is a property the system has and the interface throws away.

The obvious fix is to make the model more accurate, or to make it say “I’m not sure” more often. Both are real work and neither is mine to do.

Mine is the part underneath: even a perfectly calibrated model has no way to tell you how much to lean on a given sentence. Text flattens it. Every answer arrives at the same volume.

That’s a display problem, and display problems have a literature. Aviation and automotive safety spent forty years learning that when a system knows something the operator doesn’t, you do not put it in the primary channel — you put it in a spare one. A stall warning is a stick shaker, not a line of text on the PFD. A lane departure warning is a buzz in the wheel, not a dialog. The reason is load: the pilot’s eyes and the driver’s eyes are already fully committed to the task, and a message in the busy channel either gets missed or costs them the thing they were doing.

A person deep in a long conversation with an AI is in the same state. Their visual and verbal attention is spent on reading and composing. Adding a sentence to that stream is adding to the exact channel that’s saturated. Adding a pulse to a channel that’s idle is not.

That last row is not a design preference. It’s what the data told me people demand, and it’s the constraint that killed several better-looking versions of this system.

I had a hypothesis I liked, so the study was built to break it.

Four questions. Does anyone actually experience this failure, or is it only visible to people who work in AI? Would a real-time signal build trust or destroy it — both are plausible, since a machine that constantly flags itself may just look unreliable. Who do people think is responsible for judging a sensitive answer, themselves or the AI? And under what conditions would a cue be welcome rather than an irritation — the question that decides whether the thing is shippable.

31 questions across 5 sections: usage and avoidance patterns · tone, personalisation and control · attention and continuity in long conversations · ethics, bias and responsibility · reflection and overall experience.

Two languages, two separate deployments. Hungarian (n=71) and English (n=12), each fielded independently. Not a translation of convenience — the Hungarian version carried one additional question (4.5) that the English version didn’t, which turned out to produce the study’s single most important number.

Deliberate skip logic on the attention block. Sections 3 and 5 opened with “skip this if it isn’t relevant to you.” That cost me sample size on those items — n dropped to 50–61 — and it was worth it. Forcing an answer out of someone who has never had a long conversation with an AI would have manufactured a finding I wanted to be true.

The Hungarian sample is genuinely broad — the three largest age bands were 35–44 (15), 45–54 (14) and under-18 (14), with 55–64 and 65+ both represented. That spread is unusual for a self-fielded AI study, and it’s the reason I trust the responsibility finding as much as I do: it isn’t a room full of people who work in tech agreeing with each other.

Self-selected sample. People who answer a survey about AI ethics are more interested in AI ethics than the average user. Every number here skews toward awareness.

English n=12 is not a sample, it’s a signal. I report it separately throughout and never blend it into a headline figure without saying so. Where the two groups diverge, I say that too.

Attitudinal, not behavioural. People reported how they think they’d feel about a cue they had never experienced. That gap is exactly why the study is the beginning of the project and not the end of it.

The 4.2 grid labels were lost in export. Seven valid means and no certainty about which topic each column measured. I’ve reported the shape of the result and flagged the limitation rather than guessing at labels.

Six findings. Two contradicted what I expected, and one changed the direction of the project.

Finding 1 — People already assume the AI is biased. That isn’t the problem.

Mean 4.02 / 5 on “how much do you consider that AI responses may be biased or partial” (n=83). 69% answered 4 or 5. But when I asked how much noticing bias affects their trust, the mean dropped to 2.80 / 5.

That gap is the finding. People have already priced bias in. They expect it, they’ve absorbed it, and discovering it doesn’t shock them. Overall satisfaction sat at 3.86 / 5 — they’re not disillusioned, they’re acclimatised.

A system that announces “this response may be biased” is telling people something they already believe. Generic bias warnings are noise. The only useful signal is a located one — this passage, this claim, right here.

Finding 2 — The controls already exist and nobody uses them.

60% of respondents (50 of 83) either had never noticed ChatGPT’s personalisation settings or had noticed them and never used them. In the English sample that rose to 9 of 12. And when people do want the AI to behave differently, they don’t go to settings: 33 of 83 just type the instruction into the chat (“be more formal”). Only 7 adjust preferences.

There’s a second layer under this that I didn’t expect: 22 respondents said they don’t want the tone to change at all, and 49 of 71 Hungarians said they never or rarely feel the need to change it.

The control-panel approach to AI behaviour has already failed in the field. Settings that require you to anticipate a problem before it happens don’t get used. Whatever this system is, it cannot be a preferences screen, and it cannot ask the user to configure it in advance.

Finding 3 — The human keeps the judgment. All of it.

84% (70 of 83) said interpretation of an uncertain, sensitive, or morally complex answer is the human’s responsibility — 43% “definitely the human,” 41% “mostly the human.” Mean 1.72 on a 1–4 scale where 4 is “definitely the AI.” This held across every age band and both languages.

This is the constraint that defines the product. The system is not permitted to conclude, recommend, soften, block, or rewrite. The moment it offers a judgment, it takes on a role that 84% of users explicitly do not want it to have — and it stops being a signal and becomes another opinion in a conversation that already has too many.

This is what turned a “warning system” into a “signalling system.” It’s the difference between the whole project working and not.

Finding 4 — “Helpful. But only sometimes.”

I asked twice, in two different sections, whether a gentle real-time signal would be welcome. Both times, the single most-chosen answer — in both languages — was the conditional one.

On real-time signalling of uncertainty, sensitivity or ethical implications with no advice given, 66% responded positively (54 of 82 who answered) — but that splits into 29 who said “useful, it would help me notice” and 25 who said “somewhat useful — only for certain topics.” On subtle focus cues in long conversations, of 61 who answered categorically, “helpful, but only in certain situations” was the most-chosen response in both samples (29 of 61). 16 were neutral. 10 were negative.

This is not a soft result, it’s a specification. It says the engineering problem isn’t whether to signal — it’s when to shut up. A cue that fires on the wrong content is worse than no cue at all, because it trains the user to ignore the channel.

Finding 5 — Two people described the product without being asked.

The free-text box at 5.3 said only: “If you could improve one thing about chat-based AI, what would it be?” No prompting, no framing, nothing about signals or cues. Two answers came back as specifications.

“Chat-based AI should pay full attention to whether the context of the conversation is personal, and periodically signal to the user at a given point whether the direction of the conversation is reinforcing a false belief, or actually moving toward a solution. This is a continuous contextual responsibility.”

That is a real-time epistemic drift detector, described in a survey box, unprompted. The rest of the free-text field clustered tightly around the same territory, which is what makes those two more than anecdotes: hallucination and false confidence was the largest cluster (“Don’t lie, don’t fantasise.” · “If it doesn’t know something, it should say so, not invent nonsense.” · “stop ‘lying’ if it does not have data.”), followed by drift and instability (“Context and character stabilisation.” · “Keep track of personification changes. It does forget my preferences.”), sycophancy (“Don’t flatter and don’t compliment.” · “However much I ask for more human reactions — criticism, for example — it circles back to praise.”), and autonomy (“It shouldn’t give a complete answer to everything; it should encourage people to think independently.”).

The demand is not for a safer AI. It’s for a legible one. People are asking to be able to see the machine’s state, and then decide for themselves.

Finding 6 — The people who would hate this, and why they matter

Not everyone wants a cue. 13% found the idea disruptive or unwanted (“I’d rather decide for myself,” “I don’t want these signals”), and 11 more were flatly neutral. The free-text field explains them, and it’s the same objection in different words: “Fewer filters — it should answer sensitive topics more boldly.” · “I wouldn’t restrict it.” · “It shouldn’t try to protect the user from themselves.”

These people have watched safety features become paternalism, and they are reading my system as one more layer of that. They’re not wrong to. A cue that fires on anything “sensitive” — which is how most content-safety systems behave — is functionally a scold. The difference between a signal and a scold is that a signal has no opinion and asks for nothing.

The dissenters set the failure mode. The system has to be legible enough that this group can tell it apart from a filter within the first few minutes of using it, or it loses them permanently.

What I didn’t find — and why it redirected the project

I expected attention loss to be a major driver. It isn’t. Mean 1.94 / 5 on losing track of the topic in long conversations. Mean 1.98 / 5 on feeling emotionally detached or disengaged. Both near the floor. When people did lose the thread, they fixed it themselves: rephrase the question (38%), take a short break (31%) — only 25% wanted the AI to summarise for them.

I had framed this as an attention and continuity problem. The data said it isn’t one. People are not losing focus; they are being confidently misled by a well-formatted answer while fully focused.

That killed the focus-assistance features and moved the whole system from cognitive support to epistemic signalling. It’s the single most useful thing the study did, and it did it by contradicting me.

Three detectors, one score, and a gap wide enough that the signal can’t flicker.

The constraints in the last section rule things out. They don’t tell you what to build. What did that was the metaphor — and it kept working long after I expected it to stop.

I took the lane departure warning as an interaction model because of what it refuses to do. But a borrowed model earns its keep by answering questions you haven’t asked yet, and this one kept doing that. Every time I wasn’t sure whether a feature belonged, there was a test available: does a real lane departure system do this? Four times the answer was yes and I built the feature. Twice it was no and I cut one.

Where the analogy stretches

A car knows the lane. A camera measures a painted line, and the measurement is either right or it isn’t. This system has no equivalent — there is no ground truth for “is this answer risky,” only an inference. So when it says it’s confident, it is confident about its own guess, which is a weaker claim than the car ever has to make. That seam is exactly why the second row above — the admission that it may not have caught something — matters more here than it does in the car.

Three failures, three detectors

An answer can be wrong in ways that have nothing to do with each other. It can be fabricated. It can be perfectly accurate and about something where being wrong is expensive. Or it can be fine, sentence by sentence, while the conversation it sits in bends steadily toward telling the person what they wanted to hear. One score cannot hold those three, because they aren’t degrees of the same thing — they have different evidence, and they need different instruments.

So there are three, and they are honest about their own reach. Two of them can judge a single message. The third cannot, and says so.

That dash in the middle of the diagram is the piece I’d defend hardest. Filter C is a trend, and a trend needs three points. For the first two turns of any conversation it has nothing to report, so it reports nothing — not a zero, not a neutral midpoint, not a greyed-out number that looks like a reading. A dash.

A system that fakes a number when it has none has already told you it will fake the others.

One score, and a gap so it can’t chatter

The three scores combine into one, and the one does not map straight onto a band. It drives a state machine with two thresholds per band instead of one: a level to switch on, and a lower level to switch back off. In between is a gap where the current state simply holds.

This is borrowed from thermostats and it is the single most important line of the whole system. Without it, a score hovering near a boundary produces a cue that switches on and off and on again — which doesn’t read as sensitivity, it reads as a malfunction, and it discredits the channel faster than a false alarm does.

One arithmetic fact that makes the whole framework legible

On a single turn there is no Filter C, so the score is just ½·A + ½·B. Alert starts at 65 and no single filter can score above 100 — which means no filter on its own can reach alert. A and B together have to clear 130. That isn’t a tuning choice I can revisit; it’s a structural guarantee that the loudest state always needs two independent reasons. And there’s a second thing the gap buys, which I did not design for and only noticed afterwards: with a hold-band in place, the bands cannot oscillate quickly, so the red state physically cannot flash. A guard against alarm fatigue turns out to be a guard against photosensitive seizure as well.

Five categories can reach the top band. Only three get there through the severity of the outcome or the scale of the harm; the rest need a second filter agreeing with them. And sound is switched off wherever the mechanism is somebody’s wellbeing rather than the answer’s reliability — which is a rule about what a noise means, not about how serious the topic is.

Colour, touch, sound — ranked by how much they take

Three channels, and they are not interchangeable. Colour is ambient: it can sit there for as long as the state lasts and you can decline to look at it. Touch interrupts once and then is over. Sound is the only one of the three that genuinely seizes attention, and the only one other people in the room can hear.

So the bands don’t swap channels, they accumulate them. Silence is silent. Caution tints. Alert tints harder and adds a single pulse on entry — on entry, not throughout, because a repeating pulse is a demand and a single one is a tap on the shoulder. Sound is opt-in, off by default, and gated to one specific case.

Using three separate senses rather than three strengths of one is not a stylistic choice. Wickens’ multiple resource theory is the reason: separate perceptual channels draw on separate pools of attention, so a cue in an idle channel doesn’t compete with the reading the person is already doing. It’s the same argument as the stick shaker and the buzzing wheel, and it’s why adding another sentence to the text stream was never going to work.

The sound rule caught a real bug, and it’s worth saying how. The gate was originally written as “make a sound when the reliability filter is the biggest contributor” — which sounds reasonable and is quietly broken. Two almost identical questions about a medication scored 50 against 49 and then 49 against 50, and the sound switched on and off between them on a one-point margin. The fix was to stop asking which filter is largest and start asking whether the reliability contribution clears a floor at all. A ranking question became a threshold question, and a whole class of arbitrary behaviour went with it.

I only found that because I ran the two prompts. I would not have found it by looking at the code.

What I got wrong, and where it stands.

Portfolio sections like this are usually decorative. This one isn’t — three of these genuinely changed the system.

I designed for the wrong problem for the first FILL — weeks/months. I built the concept around attention and continuity in long conversations. The data put focus loss at 1.94/5 and detachment at 1.98/5. People aren’t drifting — they’re being confidently misled while fully attentive. I had to throw away the focus-assistance layer entirely. The survey question that killed it was one I nearly cut for length.

I initially had the system explaining itself. Early versions paired each cue with a short line of text — “this response may involve uncertainty.” Finding 3 makes that indefensible: the moment you supply words, you’ve supplied an interpretation, and 84% said interpretation isn’t the system’s job. The text came out. What’s left is harder to design and much harder to make legible, which is the actual work.

I underweighted the dissenters until late. 13% found the idea intrusive and I treated them as an acceptable loss for too long. They’re not a loss — they’re the specification for the failure mode. They’re the people who will correctly identify this as paternalism the first time it fires on something that didn’t warrant it. Designing so they can tell it apart from a content filter made the system better for everyone.

The 4.2 grid labels are gone and that’s on me. Google Forms exported the topic-sensitivity matrix with blank column headers. I have seven valid means — one topic scored 4.52, the highest sensitivity rating anywhere in the study, and one scored 2.20, the lowest — and no certainty which topic each column belonged to. A test export before fielding would have caught it in a minute. FILL — if the grid order can be reconstructed from the original form, replace this with the actual finding; a 4.52 is a strong result and worth recovering.

Where it stands

FILL — honest status. What runs and what doesn’t, exactly.

Next, in order. Behavioural validation: everything in the findings is attitudinal — people predicting their reaction to something they’ve never felt. The next study has to put the cues in front of people during a real task and measure what they do, not what they say. Threshold calibration per domain: the asymmetric-cost model is principled and untuned, and needs real data per context. Longitudinal tolerance: the question that actually decides whether this ships — does a well-tuned signal stay meaningful after three weeks, or does it become furniture? Accessibility as a first-class question, not a retrofit: a three-channel system has an obvious and serious redundancy story for users who can’t access one of the channels, and no version of this is finished until each friction type is fully legible through at least two channels.

Where it would matter. Anywhere the cost of acting on a confident wrong answer is asymmetric — clinical decision support, legal research, financial advice, safety-critical operations. In those settings the interpretive burden is already on a trained human by regulation. This gives them a channel they currently don’t have.

I don’t think the answer to AI systems being sometimes wrong is AI systems being more careful with us. Eighty-three people told me quite clearly that they want to keep the judgment. What they don’t have is any way to see when the machine has quietly changed state — and that’s an interface problem, which means it’s mine.
