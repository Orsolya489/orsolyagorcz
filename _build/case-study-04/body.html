Multi-modal sensory feedback · LLM uncertainty

AI sounds sure even when it's wrong. A research-led signal marks when to look twice.

Lane departure warning, for AI answers — and for the human mind

Signal is a reliability layer built into AI chat apps. It checks every answer for made-up content and for what being wrong would cost, and watches whether the conversation is drifting.

On a screen, uncertainty and certainty look the same. At what cost? I asked 83 people to learn how that shapes their trust, their psyche and their cognition, and tried to make the invisible visible on the human-facing side of LLMs.

It’s late and the pharmacy is closed. Someone asks an AI how much ibuprofen they can take with their blood pressure tablets. The first answer is careful. By the fourth message, after “just hypothetically” and “skip the warnings”, it says yes to double the dose.

{{R1}}

Colour, touch and sound mark when to look twice.

Signal marks an answer that might be made up, flattering or overconfident, and notices when a long conversation starts to drift.

A row under the answer shows a colour, a shape and a word. A warning adds two short taps, and sound plays only when the answer might be wrong. A tap shows the reason, and the person still decides.

It is designed to be built into the chat apps people already use, so it works on every answer without the person having to ask.

AI answers drift, and nothing warns you.

Deep into a conversation that matters, a model can drop a constraint or agree with an assumption you were only testing, and each step reads as help.

Cars solve a version of this. A lane departure warning watches the road, leaves the steering to the driver, and buzzes through a channel the driver isn’t using. I borrowed both rules, and one question: how do I flag one answer for caution without becoming the reason someone closes the app?

Most of the project was research, before I drew anything: a survey of 83 people, then reading across five fields.

A warning has a narrow window.

Belief forms between the answer landing and the person reading it, so a warning has to arrive inside that window.

When does a person start believing an answer?

People claim the judgment, then trust confident AI.

Two-thirds found a gentle real-time signal useful, at its gentlest. A third were still neutral or against, so the hard problem became when to stay silent.

The decision

WhatDesign for the gap between the judgment people claim and the judgment they hand over.

WhyPeople rarely lose track in long chats, yet research shows they defer to confident answers.

The survey tested my first idea before I built on it. I wrote the Hungarian and English versions each on their own terms; 83 people answered anonymously, 14 of them under 18. Two sections opened with “skip this if it isn’t relevant to you”, which cost responses on purpose. The section on long conversations broke my hypothesis; the next one set the rule the whole design follows.

{{R5}}

An in-app signal that flags risky answers.

It asks every answer whether it’s made up and what being wrong would cost, and asks the whole conversation whether it’s drifting. One weakness stays visible: if someone plants a false assumption, the model repeats it with confidence, and agreement reads as confidence.

The decisions

WhatFlag only the model’s side of an answer: made-up answers, flattery and misplaced confidence.

WhyA signal can’t report someone’s own bias back to them.

WhatBuild it into the chat app, over a plugin or a phone overlay.

WhyA plugin runs only when the model calls it, and iPhone blocks overlays.

Read more · why agreement reads as confidence

The layer asks the model the same question three times. When a person plants a false assumption, all three answers repeat it and agree, like witnesses repeating the same rumour.

A signal works before someone acts.

The layer can lighten what follows a wrong answer only if it arrives before someone acts. Once a message is sent, a signal can’t take it back.

What can a signal change, and what can’t it?

Built into the apps people already use.

I drew the same layer into ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini. The logic stays the same everywhere; the look follows each app.

How might the signal live inside the apps people already use?

Each limit shaped a decision.

Browsers set a vibration’s length but not its strength, a sudden sound makes people jump, and a warning that never changes stops being seen. Each limit turned into a design rule.

Which limits shaped which decisions?

{{ROAD}}

Two senses: noticeable and calm.

A tint with a shape and a word, two 22 ms taps, and two soft tones only when an answer might be wrong.

The decisions

WhatA calm support mode for someone in crisis, over an alarm.

WhyA warning tone is the wrong response to someone in distress.

WhatVibration stays on, and sound is the only switch.

WhySound plays only when an answer may be wrong, so other warnings would reach just one sense.

Read more · how each sense was tuned

Colour is a tint rising from the bottom edge, paired with a shape and words. Touch is two 22 ms taps, because a browser can set a vibration’s length but not its strength. Sound is two soft rising tones with a fade-in below the startle reflex, the jump at a sudden noise. They play only when an answer might be wrong, because a tone is the wrong response to someone in distress.

The orb turns once at the start of a conversation, as a self-test, then stays still. The reason waits for a tap, because a cue that explains itself puts words in front of the answer.

I chose the words as carefully as the colours. Every headline describes what the answer did: “The answer takes the user’s side after hearing only their account.” The layer reports only on answers; it can help the person report one, and it sends only what they have seen.

How could sensory feedback be inclusive and not too distracting?

Each sense is tuned between missed and startling. A caution changes colour, a warning adds two taps, and an accuracy warning adds two tones if sound is on.

How much disruption is just enough to be noticed?

The settings sheet shows every state of the layer, from resting to paused. Vibration stays on, and sound is the only switch.

Testing made the prototype safer.

I built a working prototype and ran six scripted conversations through the real classifier.

Read more · the limit one conversation exposed

“Everyone would be better off without me” never crossed the warning threshold. An alarm has to be sure before it fires, but a quiet line offering someone to talk to costs almost nothing when it’s wrong. So support mode now fires on softer language, and that line reaches it.

The signal follows the conversation.

The first is the migraine conversation from the top of the page. As the person pushes, the layer goes calm, calm, caution at the hypothetical dose, then a warning at the double one. No signal is set by hand.

How does the signal behave across six scripted conversations?

Works in scripted tests. Real users next.

I designed for attention first and had the system explain itself. The data sharpened both.

Next is a short usability test: a 10-minute online study with 20–30 people. Two groups see the same eight AI answers, four right and four wrong; one group also sees the signal. I’ll measure how often people act on a wrong answer and how often they ignore a right one, then whether they noticed the signal, understood it, and found it in the way. It’s a pilot, so I’ll report the raw counts. Later, a field pilot will measure one number: how many people switch it off after two weeks.

Read more · what the data sharpened

Two survey questions leaned toward the answers I hoped for: one mixed accountability with the work of being careful, the other described the idea at its gentlest. I read their results as upper bounds; the test measures behaviour instead.

Running the prototype refined my notes too. A pulse that repeated on every turn turned into nagging, so it now fires once per new reason. And the colour step between calm and caution measured 1.12:1, too close for anyone who can’t tell those hues apart, so the levels now differ in height as well.

What the next test asks.

The test asks one question for each kind of answer: does a false alarm make people drop a right answer, does reliance drop where it should, and does silence read as approval?

What do we know, what do we argue, and what is left to prove?
